This is a strong idea, and it directly neutralizes R4.O2 — which is the concern most likely to sink you with R4, since O1 is their single ranked point and O2 is the "discuss limitations/extensions" follow-through. Let me sharpen it and then flag where the risk is.
What you're actually claiming. ReCAP's selective aggregate is parameterized by NFA transitions, so it isn't wedded to left-to-right BFS. Given a regex, you can split it into sub-expressions, build a "mini-ReCAP" per fragment (each with its own NFA and selective aggregate, possibly traversed in different directions), and then compose the fragments with a merge function that reconciles the dictionaries at the join boundary. That converts R4's "your optimization only works because recursive SQL happens to grow paths one way" into "direction is a parameter of the aggregate, and composition across fragments is one extra function." You already asserted the compatibility in your rebuttal (R4.O2 response, the mini-ReCAP / reverse-is_viable_d sketch); this idea turns the assertion into a mechanism with an experiment behind it.
The merge function is the whole contribution here, so define it precisely. Your framing — "the dictionaries have to agree on a certain value" — is the right instinct, but I'd make it a typed obligation rather than a vibe. A fragment traversed in some direction produces a dictionary summarizing its sub-path, plus a set of boundary values exposed at the vertex where it will join the neighbouring fragment. The merge function takes the two boundary summaries and either (a) rejects the pair (early filter across the seam) or (b) produces the combined dictionary for the stitched path. Concretely, for monotone timestamps split into a forward fragment and a backward fragment meeting at vertex m: the forward fragment exposes max_time of its sub-path, the backward one exposes min_time of its sub-path, and merge fires the viability check fwd.max_time < bwd.min_time at the seam. That's your "increasing meets decreasing" example made rigorous — the reverse fragment maintains the mirror-image aggregate, and merge is where the two conventions are reconciled.
Two properties you'll want to state about merge, because a reviewer will ask:
Direction-duality. For a negatively-stable constraint, the reverse traversal needs the mirror aggregate/predicate (your "flip < to >"). Make explicit that this mirror is derivable, not hand-invented per query — for the constraint families in Section 5.1 (adjacent-edge predicate, monotone/distributive aggregate, set-growth), the reverse form is mechanical. If you can show the reversal is systematic for those families, the "one extra function" claim holds up; if it looks bespoke per query, R4 will say you've just moved the manual burden.
Seam-level early filtering. The payoff isn't only that composition is possible — it's that merge can prune at the seam before materializing the cross-product of the two fragments' results. That's the thing that makes this an optimization rather than a correctness afterthought, and it's exactly what a wavefront/WAVEGUIDE planner buys you (split at a rare label, explore both sides, join). State that merge preserves early filtering across the split, so the composed plan prunes at least as aggressively as the seam allows.
The experiment. This slots straight into the three-way benchmark you already proposed in the R4.O2 rebuttal (forward ReCAP vs. WAVEGUIDE-style split-and-reverse). Make the comparison isolate this mechanism: same query, same data, three plans — (i) forward-only ReCAP, (ii) hand-split fragments joined naively (materialize each side, join, then filter), (iii) fragment ReCAPs + merge with seam filtering. The claim you want the numbers to support is (iii) ≥ (i) on queries with a selective interior label, and (iii) ≫ (ii), because (ii) is what you get without the merge-level filtering. If (iii) beats (i) on the rare-interior-label case, you've shown the abstraction is load-bearing for richer plans — which is precisely the sentence that converts R4's "veneer of declarativeness" into "the structure ReCAP exposes enables plans a hand-crafted query forecloses." That single result answers O1 and O2 at once.
Now the honest risks, because you asked me to pressure-test, not cheerlead:
Correctness of composition is a genuine proof obligation, not a footnote. You're claiming that splitting the regex, traversing fragments (some reversed), and merging yields exactly the same answer set as the monolithic query. That's a theorem — regex decomposition into sub-automata whose product-with-merge equals the original product construction. For pure label regex this is standard (it's what the RPQ-splitting literature you cite in R4.O2 relies on). The novel part is that your selective aggregate also composes correctly under the split — i.e., merge of the fragment dictionaries equals what the aggregate would have computed on the whole path. You'll need at least a stated correctness condition on merge (something like: merge is associative and agrees with the monolithic update_d/is_viable_d on the concatenated path). Without that, R4 — who already caught mischaracterized related work — will treat "it composes naturally" as hand-waving.
Not every selective aggregate splits cleanly. Trail semantics (global edge-id set) is the awkward case: the two fragments each maintain a partial id set and merge must check disjointness and union them — fine, that's still a clean merge. But a constraint that's genuinely holistic (references a global property of the whole path that isn't a fold over a splittable state) may force the seam to carry the entire sub-path state, killing the pruning benefit. Be honest about which constraint families split with bounded boundary state and which don't. That honesty is itself the "limitations" discussion R4.O2 explicitly asked for — so it's not a weakness to hide, it's the content of the section.
Scope discipline. This is exciting enough that it can quietly balloon into a second contribution, and you just spent two rounds locking the revision surface with the AE. I'd frame it deliberately as: ReCAP is compatible with advanced plans; we demonstrate this via fragment composition with a merge function, and quantify it on one benchmark. A demonstration-plus-one-experiment answers O2 fully. A full theory of optimal regex splitting under selective aggregates is the follow-on paper — don't let it leak into this revision's load-bearing claims.
Where I'd want to stress it before you commit: point 1. Can you state the merge correctness condition crisply — the relationship between merge and the monolithic update_d/is_viable_d — for at least the three Section-5.1 families? If yes, this is a clean, publishable answer to O2 and probably nudges R4 off "accept not certain." If the merge condition turns out to be bespoke per query, the "one extra function, not the end of the world" framing weakens and you'd want to scope the claim down to the families where it's mechanical.

That's a fair correction, and it changes what you need to prove — downward. Let me separate the two claims you might be making, because they have very different burdens.
Claim A (strong): "Splitting regex R into fragments and merging recovers the monolithic result, and here are the conditions under which it does."
Claim B (weak/existence): "ReCAP does not foreclose fragment-based exploration; the abstraction is compatible with it."
You're right that Claim A, in full generality, isn't something the splitting literature actually proves — WAVEGUIDE and the RPQ-decomposition papers largely take "split at a rare label, join the pieces" as an operational strategy and lean on the product-construction folklore rather than a stated composition theorem. So holding yourself to a general splitting theorem would be holding yourself to a bar the prior work didn't clear either. I'll concede that.
But here's the thing — R4.O2 is only asking for Claim B. Re-read what they wrote: "How could the authors' approach be integrated with such other optimization strategies?" They are not asking you to prove splitting is correct in general or beneficial. They're asking whether your optimization is a dead-end that only works for strict left-to-right growth. So you don't need Claim A at all. You need Claim B plus a shred of evidence it's real. That's a much easier target, and your instinct — "our technique doesn't constrain you from doing this" — is exactly the right register.
So let me revise my pushback: I was pointing you at the wrong burden. You don't owe a general regex-splitting theorem. But you do still owe one narrow thing, and it's smaller than what I said before:
For the specific split you actually run in the experiment, on the specific constraint, you need the merge to be demonstrably correct — even if only by the "product construction extends to this case" argument plus empirical agreement (fragment-plus-merge returns the same answer set as forward-only ReCAP on the benchmark). That's not a general theorem; it's a worked instance plus a result-equivalence check. It's the same standard WAVEGUIDE holds itself to. If your experiment shows plan (iii) returns identical results to plan (i) and does so faster on the rare-interior-label case, you've demonstrated Claim B concretely, and nobody can ask for more than the prior art gave.
Where I'd gently hold the line: don't overstate it in the other direction either. There's a temptation, once you've established "we don't foreclose it," to also imply "…and it's better." You flagged this yourself — "we don't necessarily know if splitting is much more beneficial than left-to-right." Keep that honesty in the paper. The winning sentence is compatibility, not superiority:
ReCAP does not commit to a single exploration order. Because the selective aggregate is parameterized by NFA transitions, a fragment traversed in either direction is itself a ReCAP, and fragments compose through a merge function. Whether a given split outperforms forward exploration is a planning question orthogonal to ReCAP — the point is that ReCAP exposes the structure such plans require rather than foreclosing them.
That framing does three things at once: it answers O2, it stays inside what you can defend, and it explicitly declines to claim the splitting-is-better result you can't (and don't need to) prove. It also converts R4's "veneer of declarativeness" jab into your favor — the abstraction is what preserves the option, where a hand-crafted incremental query throws the NFA away and forecloses it (which is the argument you already made in the R4.O1 rebuttal about the last_label-uniquely-determines-state shortcut).
One caution so you don't get caught: if you assert compatibility and run an experiment, R4 will look at whether the experiment's merge is real or staged. So the merge function for the one query you demonstrate needs to actually reconcile the two fragment dictionaries (the forward max_time vs. backward min_time seam check), not be a cosmetic pass. You don't need it general; you need the one you show to be genuine. Existence proof by construction — one honest instance — is enough for Claim B.
Net: I'm walking back the "you owe a composition theorem" bar. You owe a compatibility claim, one genuinely-working merge instance, and a result-equivalence check. That's well within the revision window and it fully discharges O2. Save the general theory of when-to-split and whether-it-helps for CAMINO or the follow-on — it's not this paper's debt.
